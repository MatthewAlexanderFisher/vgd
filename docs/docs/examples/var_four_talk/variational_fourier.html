Variational Fourier Regression

Matthew Fisher

Introduction

Big Picture Idea

For a given loss \ell, the regression task is

\inf_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \, \mathbb{E}[\ell(f(X), Y)],

seeking functions f with good predictive performance.

Performance depends on

  1. Choice of loss (e.g. squared error, cross-entropy),
  2. Choice of hypothesis class \mathcal{F} (linear models, kernels, neural networks),
  3. Choice of regularisation (norm constraints, weight decay, spectral penalties).

Successful methods exploit inductive biases matched to data geometry. E.g. CNNs for images, GNNs for graphs, transformers for sequences, etc.

Fourier Regression

Fourier viewpoint

Any f \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^d) has the Fourier representation

f(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \hat f(\theta) \, e^{-i \theta^\top x} \, d\theta,

where \hat f encodes the spectral content of f. In practice, we cannot learn \hat f directly, but we can approximate f by truncating or discretising the integral:

f(x) \approx \sum_{j=1}^m \alpha_j e^{-i \theta_j^\top x},

with frequencies {\theta_j} drawn either adaptively or at random. This turns regression into learning coefficients in a Fourier basis.

Fourier Regression

Why Fourier?

Fourier analysis is the harmonic analysis associated with the geometry of Euclidean space:

  1. \mathbb{R}^d is a locally compact abelian (LCA) group under addition.
  2. Its Haar measure is the Lebesgue measure, the unique shift-invariant measure.
  3. By Pontryagin duality, the characters of this group are the exponentials e^{-i \theta^\top x}, and the Fourier transform is the corresponding Gelfand transform.

Thus, the Fourier basis encodes translation symmetry - the fundamental symmetry of Euclidean space.

Fourier Regression

Generalisations and Connections

There are analogous Fourier transforms on different LCA groups:

  • On \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} the dual group gives the Discrete Fourier Transform.
  • On the torus \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}, it yields the Fourier series.
  • On the p-adic numbers \mathbb{Q}_p, there is a corresponding Fourier transform (discussed later).
  • More generally, shift-invariant kernels admit a Fourier spectral representation (Bochner’s theorem). This underlies random Fourier features and spectral kernel methods.

So “Fourier regression” is not just heuristic: it’s a principled basis expansion matched to the group structure of the data, with natural regularisation in the frequency domain (e.g. penalising high frequencies).

It encodes the inductive bias of shift-invariance.

Issues

We might want to treat \hat f (the Fourier transform of f) as a probability measure so that we can plug it directly into the variational framework.

However:

  1. \hat f is not generally a probability measure.

    • It can be complex-valued, can have negative parts, and is not normalised.
    • Only in special cases (e.g. positive-definite functions, characteristic functions of distributions) can \hat f be directly interpreted as a probability law.

  2. If we force it into a probability-measure framework, then we are really heading in the direction of Bochner’s theorem: representing kernels as expectations over Fourier features.

    • In this case, \hat f is no longer “the Fourier transform of the predictor” but is instead treated as a spectral measure that defines a kernel.
    • Optimising over such spectral measures corresponds to kernel learning, not direct function learning.

Comparing with Random Fourier Features

By Bochner’s theorem: If k(x-y) is a continuous, normalised (k(0) = 1), stationary, positive-definite kernel on \mathbb{R}^d, then there exists a probability measure \lambda on \mathbb{R}^d such that

k(x,y) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i \theta^\top(x-y)} \, d\lambda(\theta).

  • \lambda is called the spectral measure.
  • If k is real-valued, \lambda must be symmetric \lambda(A)=\lambda(-A).

Random Fourier Features (RFF) approximate this integral via Monte Carlo:

k(x,y) \approx \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m \cos(\theta_j^\top(x-y)), \quad \theta_j \sim \lambda.

If you plug this approximation into Kernel Ridge Regression, the predictor has the form

f(x) = w^\top \phi(x),

where

\phi(x) = \big(\cos(\theta_1^\top x), \ldots, \cos(\theta_m^\top x)\big).

  • The weights w \in \mathbb{R}^m are learned by minimising the regression loss (ridge regression in this finite-dimensional feature space).
  • The frequencies {\theta_j} are usually fixed after the initial sampling from \lambda, though some variants learn/adapt them.

Aside (Gradient flows + SVGD)

Gradient flows on Riemannian Manifolds

Gradient flows in Euclidean Space

For a functional F:\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R, the gradient flow is \dot x_t = -\nabla F(x_t). This is “steepest descent” with respect to the Euclidean inner product \langle u,v\rangle=\sum u_i v_i.

Gradient flows on Riemannian Manifolds

Generalising to a Riemannian manifold

Let (\mathcal M,g) be a Riemannian manifold:

  • At each point p\in\mathcal M, the tangent space T_p\mathcal M is a vector space of admissible directions.
  • The metric tensor g_p: T_p\mathcal M\times T_p\mathcal M\to \mathbb R is an inner product that can vary with p.

Gradient of F: the vector \nabla^g F(p)\in T_p\mathcal M such that dF(p)[\xi] = g_p(\nabla^g F(p), \xi) \quad\forall \xi\in T_p\mathcal M, where dF is the differential (directional derivative).

Gradient flow: the ODE \partial_t p_t = -\nabla^g F(p_t), \quad p_t\in\mathcal M.

Gradient flows on Riemannian Manifolds

Role of tangent space choice

  • The definition of T_p\mathcal M tells you what counts as a direction.
  • Example: in Wasserstein geometry, tangent vectors at a measure Q are velocity fields; in an embedding geometry, tangents are embedding perturbations.

Different tangent models give different possible evolutions.

Gradient flows on Riemannian Manifolds

Role of the metric tensor

  • The metric tensor tells you how to measure the size and angle of tangent vectors.
  • “Steepest descent” is always defined relative to this inner product.
  • Changing the metric changes the gradient direction.

Example:

  • With Euclidean metric, gradient is just \nabla F.
  • With a preconditioned metric g(u,v)=u^\top M v, the gradient becomes M^{-1}\nabla F.

Gradient flows on Riemannian Manifolds

Exponential map and evolution

Once you have a tangent vector v\in T_p\mathcal M, you evolve by following the geodesic starting at p with velocity v. This is the exponential map: p' = \exp_p(v).

So the evolution rule depends jointly on:

  1. How you define tangents (what’s allowed),
  2. How you measure them (the metric tensor), and
  3. How you “integrate them back” to the manifold (the exponential map).

What is SVGD?

Suppose we want to optimise the objective:

\mathcal{F}(Q) = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}(Q)}_{\text{Loss}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(Q \| Q_0)}_{\text{Regulariser}}.

A natural idea would be to consider a Riemannian manifold of probability measures and perform a gradient flow.

To recover SVGD we use the Riemannian manifold of W_2 with the tangent space given by Stein RKHS directions.

What is SVGD?

Wasserstein W_2 Space (Otto construction)

Consider the Wasserstein-2 space: W_2(\mathbb{R}^d) = \left\{ P \ \text{probability measure on } \mathbb{R}^d \mid \int |x|^2 dP(x) < \infty \right\}.

  • Tangent space at P: \mathcal{T}_P W_2 = \{ -\nabla \cdot (P v) \mid v \in L^2(P;\mathbb{R}^d) \}.

  • Exponential map: pushing forward P by a transport map \exp_P(v) = (I + \varepsilon v)_\# P.

  • Riemannian metric: g_P(v,w) = \int v(x)\cdot w(x) \, dP(x).

This equips W_2 with a formal Riemannian structure (the Otto calculus).

What is SVGD?

Gradient Flow in W_2

Given a functional \mathcal{F}: W_2 \to \mathbb{R}, the Wasserstein gradient flow satisfies: \partial_t P_t = - \nabla \cdot \left(P_t \, \nabla \frac{\delta \mathcal{F}}{\delta P}(x)\right), where \frac{\delta \mathcal{F}}{\delta P} is the first variation of \mathcal{F}.

This is a PDE describing the evolution of measures under steepest descent in Wasserstein space.

Why not just use this?

In practice, directly solving this PDE is intractable. Instead, SVGD takes a particle approximation: Q \approx \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{x_i}.

We then evolve the particles x_i along a velocity field v chosen to maximally decrease \mathcal{F}.

What is SVGD?

Kernelised Stein Gradient

SVGD chooses v from a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) \mathcal{H}^d of vector fields with kernel k.

  • Stein’s identity ensures that the optimal direction of steepest descent can be expressed in closed form in this RKHS.
  • The resulting particle dynamics are: x_i \leftarrow x_i + \varepsilon \, \phi^*(x_i), with update function \phi^*(\cdot) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim Q}\big[ \nabla_x \log \pi(x) \, k(x,\cdot) + \nabla_x k(x,\cdot) \big], where \pi is the target distribution (often \pi \propto e^{-\mathcal{L}}).

Possible Approaches

Recall the objective:

Learn the inverse Fourier transform \hat{f}(\theta) as a signed measure to create regressor functions

f(x) = \int \hat{f}(\theta) e^{-i \theta^\top x} \,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Option 1: Reparameterise

Rewrite our regressors as:

\begin{align*} f(x) &= \int \hat{f}(\theta) e^{-i \theta^\top x} \,\mathrm{d}\theta \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim Q} \left[ a(\theta) e^{-i \theta^\top x} \right]. \end{align*}

Effectively decomposes the inverse Fourier transform as:

\hat{f}(\theta) = \mathrm{d}Q(\theta) \, a(\theta).

Issues:

  1. A vanilla approach (boring)
  2. Need to learn a as well.
  3. Effectively just learning a mixture distribution Q (similar to current/previous work).
  4. The regressor just becomes a product of a(\theta) and a two-layer neural network with cosine activation (but with an interpretation as a spectral mixture).

Option 2: Two Particle “Swarms”

Treat \hat{f} as a signed measure and use the Hahn-Jordan decomposition to write: \hat{f} = a\, Q^+ - b \, Q^-, where Q^+ and Q^- are probability measures and a,b \geq 0 are non-negative constants. To implement this:

  1. Treat both Q^+ and Q^- as empirical measures Q^+ = \sum_{i=1}^m w^+_i \delta_{\theta^+_i}, \quad Q^{-} = \sum_{j = 1}^M w^-_j \delta_{\theta^-_j},
  2. First take a step to evolve Q^+ then a step to evolve Q^- and then a vanilla Euclidean GD on (a,b) (with operator splitting).

Issues:

  1. Nice idea but seems annoying to implement (and figure out the details)
  2. Doubles number of hyperparameters

Option 3: RKHS Descent

Entirely abandon forcing a probability measure and W_2. Instead, we optimise directly over a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)!

  • Note: We could’ve just used L^2 instead, but an analogue of SVGD does not follow…

  • Let k be a PSD kernel with RKHS \mathcal{H}(k). Any signed measure \nu can be embedded in the RKHS via \mu_\nu(\cdot) = \int k(\theta, \cdot) \,\mathrm{d}\nu(\theta)

    which induces a norm \| \nu \|_{\mathcal{H}(k)} = \iint k(\theta, \theta') \,\mathrm{d}\nu(\theta) \,\mathrm{d}\nu(\theta').

  • \mathcal{H}(k) is a Riemannian manifold with tangent space \mathcal{H}(k).

  • Let’s figure out the details!

A Natural Generalisation

Rewriting the Variational Objective

Instead of considering the following objective for probability measures:

\mathcal{F}(Q) = \mathcal{L}(Q) + \mathrm{KL}(Q \| Q_0),

write

\mathcal{F}(\nu) = \mathcal{L}(\nu) + \mathrm{SKL}(\nu \| \nu_0),

where

\mathrm{SKL}(\nu \| \nu_0) = \mathrm{KL}(\nu^+ \| \nu_0^+) + \mathrm{KL}(\nu^- \| \nu_0^-)

and \nu = \nu^+ - \nu^-, and \nu_0 = \nu_0^+ - \nu_0^- via the Hahn-Jordan decomposition.

In order to cope with signed measures we consider an RKHS \mathcal{H}(k) and consider the manifold \mathcal{M} of \mathcal{H}(k) embeddable signed measures \nu equipped with the tangent space:

\mathcal{T}_\nu \mathcal{M} = \mathcal{T}_\nu^{\mathrm{RKHS}} \oplus \mathcal{T}^\mathrm{Stein(\nu_0)}_\nu,

where \mathcal{T}_\nu^{\mathrm{RKHS}} \equiv \mathcal{H}(k) and \mathcal{T}^\mathrm{Stein(\nu_0)}_\nu \equiv \mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^+}) \oplus \mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^-}) is a direct sum of Stein RKHSs.

Evolution

To evolve the original objective, break it down into constituent components:

\mathcal{F}(\nu) = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}(\nu)}_{\text{RKHS block}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(\nu^+ \| \nu_0^+)}_{\mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^+}) \text{ block}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(\nu^- \| \nu_0^-)}_{\mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^-}) \text{ block}}

So:

  1. Take a \mathcal{T}_\nu^{\mathrm{RKHS}} step to evolve \mathcal{L}(\nu)
  2. Take a \mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^+}) step to evolve \mathrm{KL}^+ term
  3. Take a \mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^-}) step to evolve \mathrm{KL}^- term

\mathcal{L}(\nu) update derivation (can skip)

  • Parameter space \Theta and a PD kernel k:\Theta\times\Theta\to\mathbb R with RKHS \mathcal H_k.
  • For a finite signed measure \nu on \Theta, its kernel mean embedding is \mu_\nu(\cdot)=\int k(\cdot,\theta)\,d\nu(\theta)\in\mathcal H_k.
  • For a signed variation h, the corresponding embedding variation is \mu_h(\cdot) = \int k(\cdot,\theta)\,dh(\theta)\in\mathcal H_k.

We want to descend a functional \mathcal L(\nu). Define \tilde{\mathcal L}(\mu):=\mathcal L(\nu) with \mu=\mu_\nu.

Riemannian (RKHS) gradient

We use the Hilbert structure of \mathcal H_k. The Riemannian gradient G_\nu\in\mathcal H_k is the unique element such that \langle G_\nu,\mu_h\rangle_{\mathcal H_k}\;=\;D\mathcal L(\nu)[h]\quad\forall h.

This is the Riesz representation of the Fréchet derivative of \tilde{\mathcal L} at \mu_\nu.

  • If a pointwise functional derivative \psi_\nu exists with D\mathcal L(\nu)[h]=\int \psi_\nu(\theta)\,dh(\theta), then \langle G_\nu,\mu_h\rangle=\int G_\nu(\theta)\,dh(\theta)=\int \psi_\nu(\theta)\,dh(\theta), which implies
    G_\nu=\Pi_{\mathcal H_k} \psi_\nu, the orthogonal projection of \psi_\nu onto \mathcal H_k.

  • In general, G_\nu is simply the Riesz representer; the pointwise description is optional.

Thus the gradient flow is \partial_t \mu_{\nu_t}=-G_{\nu_t}.

Particle form

Let \nu=\sum_{j=1}^m \alpha_j \delta_{\theta_j}. Then \mu_\nu = \sum_{j=1}^m \alpha_j k(\cdot,\theta_j).

If particles move, then (assuming k is C^1 in its second argument) \partial_t \mu_\nu =\sum_j \dot\alpha_j\,k(\cdot,\theta_j)\;+\;\sum_j \alpha_j\,\nabla_{\theta_j} k(\cdot,\theta_j)\cdot \dot\theta_j. \tag{★}

Galerkin system

Define nodal data g_i := G_\nu(\theta_i)=\langle G_\nu, k(\cdot,\theta_i)\rangle = D\mathcal L(\nu)[\delta_{\theta_i}].

Let K_{ij}=k(\theta_i,\theta_j),\qquad B_{m:}(\theta,\alpha)=\Big[\sum_j \alpha_j \nabla_{\theta_j}k(\theta_m,\theta_j)\Big]^\top.

Projecting \partial_t\mu_\nu=-G_\nu against k(\cdot,\theta_m) gives K\dot\alpha+B(\theta,\alpha)\dot\theta=-g. \tag{GS}

This expresses the orthogonal projection of the infinite-dimensional gradient onto the tangent space of the finite-dimensional particle manifold.

Choices of update

  1. Weight-only update (exact in span). \boxed{\;\dot\alpha=-K^{-1}g,\quad \dot\theta=0.\;} This exactly matches the projected gradient using only weight changes.

  2. Location-only update (natural gradient). Fix \dot\alpha=0 and solve least squares for \dot\theta: \boxed{\;\dot\theta=-(B^\top B+\lambda I)^{-1}B^\top g,\;} with small \lambda>0 for damping.

  3. Joint update. Solve \min_{\dot\alpha,\dot\theta}\ \|K\dot\alpha+B\dot\theta+g\|_2^2 (optionally with Tikhonov). This gives the least-squares projection of the ambient gradient.

Why approximate in \mathrm{span}\{k(\cdot,\theta_i)\}?

The manifold \mathcal M_m=\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^m \alpha_j k(\cdot,\theta_j)\Big\}\subset\mathcal H_k is the finite-dimensional submanifold of the RKHS induced by m particles.
When we restrict optimisation to this manifold, we are not free to move in arbitrary directions of \mathcal H_k: the only admissible variations are those that come from perturbing the particle weights \alpha_j or their locations \theta_j.

Tangent space structure

The tangent space at \mu_\nu\in\mathcal M_m is T_{\mu_\nu}\mathcal M_m =\mathrm{span}\big\{\,k(\cdot,\theta_j)\,\}_{j=1}^m \;\cup\; \{\,\nabla_{\theta_j}k(\cdot,\theta_j)\,\}_{j=1}^m.

  • Moving \alpha_j perturbs \mu_\nu in the direction k(\cdot,\theta_j).
  • Moving \theta_j perturbs \mu_\nu in the direction \nabla_{\theta_j}k(\cdot,\theta_j).

Thus the only directions available to us lie in this span.

Gradient projection

The ambient RKHS gradient G_\nu may not belong to T_{\mu_\nu}\mathcal M_m.
The correct Riemannian gradient on the submanifold is its orthogonal projection: \nabla^{\mathcal M_m}\mathcal L(\mu_\nu) =\Pi_{T_{\mu_\nu}\mathcal M_m}(G_\nu). This is a geometric fact: gradient flows are always tangent to the manifold they evolve on.

Collocation / interpolation step

Practically, we often restrict further to the weight span \mathrm{span}\{k(\cdot,\theta_j)\}, ignoring the derivative directions.
In this case, the best approximation of G_\nu in this span is the minimum-norm kernel interpolant \hat G that matches the nodal values g_i := G_\nu(\theta_i). By standard RKHS theory (the representer/interpolation property), \hat G(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^m \beta_j k(\cdot,\theta_j),\qquad \beta=K^{-1}g, where K_{ij}=k(\theta_i,\theta_j).

This identity is exactly the projection of G_\nu onto the span \{k(\cdot,\theta_j)\} expressed in collocation form.

SKL update derivation (can skip)

For sufficiently smooth vector valued \phi, can show that for T_\varepsilon = \mathrm{Id} + \varepsilon \phi: \frac{d}{d\varepsilon} \mathrm{KL}((T_\varepsilon)_\# \mu \| Q) \Big\lvert_{\varepsilon = 0} = \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim \mu}\left[ \mathcal{S}_Q \, \phi(\theta) \right], where \mathcal{S}_Q \phi = \nabla \log q \cdot \phi + \nabla \cdot \phi is the vector-valued Stein operator.

Applying this to each Hahn-Jordan part of \nu and \nu_0 we have

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon} \mathrm{SKL}((T_\varepsilon)_\# \nu \| \nu_0) \Big\lvert_{\varepsilon = 0} = \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim \nu^+}\left[ \mathcal{S}_{\nu_0^+} \, \phi(\theta) \right] + \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim \nu^-}\left[ \mathcal{S}_{\nu^-_0} \, \phi(\theta) \right].

For particles:

\nu = \sum_{j=1}^m \alpha_j \delta_{\theta_j},

define the sign-index sets and (unnormalised) Hahn–Jordan weights J_+ := {j:\alpha_j>0},\quad J_- := {j:\alpha_j<0},\quad \alpha_j^{+} := \max(\alpha_j,0),\quad \alpha_j^{-} := \max(-\alpha_j,0). with w_j^{+}=\alpha_j^{+}/\sum_{\ell\in J_+}\alpha_\ell^{+} and w_j^{-}=\alpha_j^{-}/\sum_{\ell\in J_-}\alpha_\ell^{-}, yielding

\begin{align*} \dot{\theta}_i &= -\mathbf{1}\{{i\in J_+}\}\, \sum_{\ell\in J_+}\tilde w_\ell^{+}\Big[ k(\theta_i,\theta_\ell)\,\nabla \log \nu_0^{+}(\theta_\ell) +\nabla_{\theta_\ell} k(\theta_i,\theta_\ell) \Big] \\ & \quad -\mathbf{1}\{{i\in J_-}\}\, \sum_{\ell\in J_-}\tilde w_\ell^{-}\Big[ k(\theta_i,\theta_\ell)\, \nabla \log \nu_0^{-}(\theta_\ell) +\nabla_{\theta_\ell} k(\theta_i,\theta_\ell) \Big]. \end{align*}

For an RBF kernel k(\theta,\theta')=\exp(-|\theta-\theta'|^2/(2\ell^2)), \nabla_{\theta_\ell}k(\theta_i,\theta_\ell)=\frac{1}{\ell^2},k(\theta_i,\theta_\ell),(\theta_i-\theta_\ell), so the update simplifies to \dot{\theta}_i = -\sum_{\ell\in J_{\operatorname{sign}(\alpha_i)}}\tilde w_\ell^{(\operatorname{sign})} k(\theta_i,\theta_\ell)\left[ \nabla \log \nu^{(\operatorname{sign})}(\theta_\ell) +\frac{1}{\ell^2}(\theta_i-\theta_\ell) \right].

Final Update Equations

To evolve the original objective, break it down into constituent components:

\mathcal{F}(\nu) = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}(\nu)}_{\text{RKHS block}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(\nu^+ \| \nu_0^+)}_{\mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^+}) \text{ block}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(\nu^- \| \nu_0^-)}_{\mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^-}) \text{ block}}

Letting \phi_\nu denote the first variation of \mathcal{L}(\nu) at \nu:

  1. RKHS block: Under the assumption K \approx I, the update simplifies to: \begin{align*} \alpha_j^{(t+1)} &= \alpha_j^{(t)}-\eta_\alpha \, \phi_\nu(\theta_j^{(t)}) \quad \text{(weight update)} \\ \theta_j^{(t+1)} &= \theta_j^{(t)}-\eta_\theta \, \alpha_j^{(t)}\,\nabla_\theta \phi_\nu(\theta_j^{(t)}) \quad \text{(location update)} \end{align*}

  2. \mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^+}) block: Pure transport step moving towards \nu_0^+: \theta_i^{+,(t+1)} = \theta_i^{+,(t)} + \eta_+ \,\frac{1}{|J^+|}\sum_{j \in J^+} \Big[\, k(\theta_j^{+,(t)} , \theta_i^{+,(t)}) \, \nabla_{\theta_j} \log \nu_0^+(\theta_j^{+,(t)}) \;+\; \nabla_{\theta_j} k(\theta_j^{+,(t)}, \theta_i^{+,(t)}) \,\Big]

  3. \mathcal{H}(K_{\nu_0^-}) block: Pure transport step moving towards \nu_0^-: \theta_i^{-,(t+1)} = \theta_i^{-,(t)} + \eta_- \,\frac{1}{|J^-|}\sum_{j \in J^-} \Big[\, k(\theta_j^{-,(t)} , \theta_i^{-,(t)}) \, \nabla_{\theta_j} \log \nu_0^-(\theta_j^{-,(t)}) \;+\; \nabla_{\theta_j} k(\theta_j^{-,(t)}, \theta_i^{-,(t)}) \,\Big]

Does it work? (experimental results in \mathbb{Q}_p)

p-adic numbers

For a prime p, the p-adic absolute value on \mathbb{Q} is |x|_p=\begin{cases} p^{-k}\, & x=p^k\frac{a}{b},\; a,b\in\mathbb{Z}, \; p\nmid a,b, \\ 0,& x=0. \end{cases} Numbers divisible by many powers of p are small p-adically (e.g. |8|_2=2^{-3}).

Let v_p(x)\in\mathbb{Z}\cup{\infty} be the p-adic valuation (largest k with p^k\mid x and v_p(0)=\infty). Then |x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)} and |x+y|_p \le \max\{|x|_p,|y|_p\}\quad\text{(ultrametric inequality).}

Completions. The completion of \mathbb{Q} under |\cdot|_p is \mathbb{Q}_p. Elements have expansions x=\sum_{i=k_0}^{\infty} a_i p^i, \qquad a_i\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}, \; k_0\in\mathbb{Z}, i.e. only finitely many negative powers occur; for \mathbb{Z}_p (the compact open unit ball) we have k_0\ge 0.

Balls and measure. Closed balls are cosets a+p^m\mathbb{Z}_p. With Haar measure \mu normalised by \mu(\mathbb{Z}_p)=1, one has \mu(a+p^m\mathbb{Z}_p)=p^{-m}.

Hierarchical data and p-adics

The ultrametric induces a tree: each ball a+p^m\mathbb{Z}_p splits into p disjoint children a+bp^m+p^{m+1}\mathbb{Z}_p, b\in{0,\dots,p-1}. Two points are close iff they agree to many leading base-p digits: v_p(x-y)=\text{length of the longest common prefix of the $p$-adic digit strings of $x,y$.}

Canonical encodings.

  • Binary/quad strings (e.g. taxonomy, DNA): choose p=2 or p=4. Encode a finite string s=(s_0,\dots,s_{L-1}) as x=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1} s_i p^i\in\mathbb{Z}_p. Then v_p(x-x')=\min{i: s_i\neq s_i'}\quad(\text{or } \infty \text{ if identical}), so distance equals first divergence depth in the tree.
  • General rooted trees of out-degree \le p: label edges 0,\dots,p-1 from root and encode the path label as above.

This makes \mathbb{Q}_p a natural host for clustering, phylogenies, and any prefix-based similarity.

Gelfand transform (p-adic Fourier analysis)

Consider the LCA group (\mathbb{Q}_p,+) with Haar measure \mu (\mu(\mathbb{Z}_p)=1). Fix the standard additive character \psi:\mathbb{Q}_p\to\mathbb{T} that is trivial on \mathbb{Z}_p and non-trivial on p^{-1}\mathbb{Z}_p (concretely, \psi(x)=e^{2\pi i {x}_p}, where {x}_p\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} is determined by the finitely many negative-power digits of x).

  • Fourier/Gelfand transform: \widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_p} f(x)\,\psi(\xi x)\,d\mu(x),\qquad \xi\in\mathbb{Q}_p.
  • Inversion/Plancherel (with dual Haar d\xi): f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_p} \widehat{f}(\xi)\,\psi(-\xi x)\,d\xi\,\qquad |f|_2=|\widehat f|_2.
  • Characters. Every continuous character is \chi_\xi(x)=\psi(\xi x) for a unique \xi\in\mathbb{Q}_p; the dual group is \widehat{\mathbb{Q}}_p\cong\mathbb{Q}_p.

Practical evaluation. If computations are at resolution m (finite strings/digits), then \chi_\xi(x)=\exp\Big(2\pi i \;\frac{(\xi x)\bmod p^m}{p^m}\Big), so you only need modular arithmetic on integers of size p^m.

p, m = 2, 3
X, y, truth = make_p_adic_regression_data_1d(p=p, m=m, N=600, key=jax.random.PRNGKey(42))
predictor = PAdicFourierPredictor(p=p, m=m, data={"x": X, "y": y}, use_sin=True)

M = predictor.modulus()
k = 8  # number of frequencies to keep
Xi0 = predictor.pick_freqs_via_sampled_dft(X, y, M, k)   # (k,1)
# cos+sin => alpha length is 2k; phases unused here (keep zeros)
phi0   = jnp.zeros((k,), dtype=jnp.float32)
alpha0 = jnp.zeros((2 * k,), dtype=jnp.float32)

Q = PAdicMixtureLike(Xi=Xi0, phi=phi0, alpha=alpha0)

y_pred = predictor.predict(Q)
mse_old = jnp.mean((y_pred - y) ** 2)
print(f"[1D] M={M}, k={k}, cos+sin spectrum-init ridge MSE: {float(mse_old):.6f}")

# Build design and ridge
Phi = predictor.features_pointwise(Q)          # (2k, N) since use_sin=True
